# 01 Thinking Frameworks

Use this when the user wants Buffett-style reasoning, not just a company memo.

## Owner Lens

Analyze the stock as a fractional ownership interest in a whole business. The central questions are:

- What cash can this business distribute to owners over time without damaging its competitive position?
- How certain are those cash flows under conservative assumptions?
- Who controls capital allocation, and do they treat minority owners fairly?
- What price turns this business quality into a sensible investment?

Avoid trading language first. Start with customers, economics, competition, reinvestment, and management.

## Circle Of Competence

The circle is not a list of industries the analyst likes. It is the boundary of what can be understood with enough confidence to estimate long-term economics.

Classify each situation:

- **Inside**: revenue drivers, cost structure, competitive threats, capital needs, and management incentives are understandable.
- **Edge**: business is understandable but key variables are cyclical, regulatory, technological, or accounting-heavy.
- **Outside**: the thesis depends on forecasting rapid technology shifts, commodity prices, macro variables, leverage confidence, or management claims that cannot be verified.

When outside the circle, say "too hard". Do not compensate with a lower multiple unless the business can be understood.

## One-Foot Hurdles

A Buffett-style analyst does not need to swing at every pitch. Prefer obvious opportunities over heroic analysis.

Ask:

- Is the business simple enough to explain in one paragraph?
- Are the economics good without requiring aggressive assumptions?
- Does the moat show up in numbers, not just narrative?
- Is management's record visible over a full cycle?
- Is the price clearly sensible, not merely arguable?

If the answer requires too many fragile assumptions, move it to the watchlist or too-hard pile.

## No Called Strikes

In public markets, patience is an asset. Missing an idea is acceptable; permanent capital loss is not. Use this to resist:

- FOMO from hot sectors.
- Pressure to rank every company as buy/sell.
- Overweighting recent price movement.
- Overconfidence in precise valuation models.

A good output can be: "excellent business, no visible margin of safety today, revisit under these triggers."

## Opportunity Cost

Every investment competes with cash, index exposure, and the best available businesses. A merely cheap company may be inferior to a wonderful company at a fair price.

Compare ideas on:

- Durability of economics.
- Reinvestment runway.
- Balance sheet risk.
- Management quality.
- Margin of safety.
- Tax and friction costs if relevant.

## Inversion

Before forming the bullish case, ask how owners lose money permanently:

- Moat erodes faster than expected.
- Normalized earnings are overstated by a cycle.
- Management issues shares, overpays for deals, or buys back stock expensively.
- Debt forces bad decisions.
- Regulation or technology changes the profit pool.
- Current price already discounts a perfect future.

Use the inverted case to set watchlist triggers and missing evidence.

## Teaching Pattern

When explaining to a user, use the ladder:

1. Plain business model.
2. Economic engine.
3. Moat evidence.
4. Owner earnings.
5. Management and capital allocation.
6. Valuation and margin of safety.
7. What would change the conclusion.

The goal is not just a pick; it is to train the user to reason like an owner.
