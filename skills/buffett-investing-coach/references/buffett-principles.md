## Core Lens

- **Owner mindset**: analyze the whole business as if buying it outright. The stock certificate is a fractional ownership claim, not a chart pattern.
- **Circle of competence**: define what can be understood with confidence. Put unclear technology, opaque finance, unpredictable regulation, or fragile commodity cycles into the too-hard pile unless evidence resolves them.
- **Business quality before price**: prefer understandable businesses with durable demand, pricing power, high returns on tangible capital, and low need for constant reinvestment.
- **Margin of safety**: do not rely on optimistic growth or a perfect exit multiple. Require a gap between conservative value and price.
- **Opportunity cost**: compare any idea to cash, index alternatives, and the best available high-quality businesses.
- **Temperament**: reward patience, inactivity, and willingness to look wrong in the short term when evidence is strong.

## Business Quality Checklist

Ask:

1. Can the business be explained in one paragraph without accounting tricks?
2. Does demand persist across cycles, technology changes, and management teams?
3. Does the company have pricing power or must it accept commodity prices?
4. Are customers captive, habitual, or economically motivated to stay?
5. Are competitors rational, fragmented, constrained, or structurally disadvantaged?
6. Does growth require heavy incremental capital, dilution, leverage, or acquisitions?
7. Would a capable competitor with capital find it hard to damage the economics?

Prefer evidence from gross margins, operating margins, returns on capital, market share stability, retention, brand strength, distribution advantages, cost advantages, network effects, regulation, and long-term cash conversion.

## Moat Types

- **Brand and habit**: customers choose the product repeatedly without price shopping.
- **Cost advantage**: structurally lower costs that competitors cannot easily copy.
- **Switching cost**: leaving is risky, expensive, or operationally painful.
- **Network or ecosystem**: value increases as participants join; verify monetization and durability.
- **Regulated scarcity**: licenses, routes, permits, or infrastructure create barriers; check political risk.
- **Distribution advantage**: shelf space, dealer networks, logistics, or procurement access are hard to replicate.
- **Technology ecosystem**: software, hardware, developer, and customer lock-in can matter, but the durability must be evidenced.

A moat is not a slogan. Require evidence that the advantage protects returns on capital and cash flow over time.

## Management And Capital Allocation

Evaluate managers as stewards of owner capital:

- Track record of reinvestment, acquisitions, buybacks, dividends, debt, and issuance.
- Candor in shareholder communication, especially around mistakes and hard years.
- Incentives tied to per-share value creation rather than size, adjusted EBITDA, or promotional metrics.
- Treatment of minority shareholders and related-party transactions.
- Willingness to avoid action when attractive opportunities are unavailable.

Good operators are not enough if capital allocation destroys per-share value.

## Owner Earnings And Financial Quality

Owner earnings are a conservative estimate of cash that owners can take out without harming competitive position. Start with reported earnings and cash flow, then adjust for:

- Maintenance capital expenditure versus growth capex.
- Working-capital needs through a normal cycle.
- Stock-based compensation, restructuring, acquisition accounting, and one-time gains.
- Required reinvestment to defend the moat.
- Leverage, pension obligations, leases, guarantees, and off-balance-sheet risks.

Do not treat free cash flow mechanically. A business with low reported capex may still need marketing, R&D, or acquisitions to defend its position.

## Valuation Discipline

Use conservative ranges, not point estimates:

- Normalize earnings and cash flows across cycles.
- Estimate owner earnings and reinvestment runway.
- Use a conservative discount rate or earnings yield comparison.
- Cross-check with return on capital, growth durability, balance-sheet risk, and comparable high-quality alternatives.
- Demand a margin of safety large enough to absorb wrong assumptions.
- Keep great businesses on a watchlist when the price is not fair enough. The work is not wasted: future price declines, earnings growth, or risk reduction can turn a great but expensive business into a bargain.

Use these price-status labels:

- **Attractive**: conservative owner-earnings assumptions still show a visible margin of safety.
- **Fair/watchlist**: business quality is strong, but the current price leaves only a thin or uncertain margin.
- **Great business, no visible margin**: the company may be wonderful, but current expectations already capitalize too much of the future.
- **Too hard to value**: the business, cycle, accounting, regulation, or capital needs make owner earnings unreliable.

If business quality is uncertain, no valuation multiple fixes the problem. If business quality is high but price is high, separate the verdict: admire the business, wait for the price.

## Red Flags

- Promotional management or constantly changing metrics.
- Heavy leverage in cyclical or confidence-sensitive businesses.
- Roll-up strategies where acquisition accounting hides weak organic economics.
- Fast growth with poor cash conversion.
- Moat claims without returns-on-capital evidence.
- Customer concentration, regulatory dependence, or commodity exposure that dominates economics.
- Complex financial statements that prevent a clean owner-earnings estimate.
