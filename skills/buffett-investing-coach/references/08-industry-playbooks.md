# 08 Industry Playbooks

Use this when adapting the Buffett-style framework to a sector. Industry playbooks are starting points, not shortcuts.

## Consumer Brands

Look for:

- Habitual repeat purchase.
- Pricing power without volume collapse.
- Distribution strength.
- Low capital intensity.
- Global runway.

Risks: private label, changing tastes, retailer power, marketing spend needed to defend share.

## Insurance

Look for:

- Underwriting discipline over cycles.
- Low or negative cost of float.
- Conservative reserving.
- Investment discipline.
- Culture that rejects dumb volume.

Risks: under-reserving, catastrophe concentration, social inflation, yield pressure, leverage.

Required metrics before a confident conclusion:

- Combined ratio and trend.
- Underwriting profit versus reserve release support.
- Reserve adequacy and adverse development.
- Float cost and investment portfolio risk.
- Catastrophe exposure and reinsurance dependence.

Hard stop:

- If combined ratio, reserve adequacy, or underwriting quality cannot be sourced and explained, default to `too hard` or `insufficient evidence`.

## Banks And Financials

Look for:

- Low-cost deposits or funding advantage.
- Conservative credit culture.
- Simple balance sheet.
- Strong capital ratios.
- Fee businesses with customer stickiness.

Risks: leverage opacity, duration mismatch, credit cycle, regulatory constraints, aggressive growth.

Required metrics before a confident conclusion:

- CET1 or equivalent regulatory capital.
- Deposit mix, funding concentration, and deposit beta where relevant.
- Net interest margin and sensitivity.
- Unrealized losses or AOCI exposure where relevant.
- Reserve coverage, credit quality, and charge-off trend.
- Duration mismatch or other balance-sheet fragility.

Hard stop:

- If capital, funding, credit, or duration metrics are missing, do not force an owner-earnings style conclusion. Default to `too hard` or `insufficient evidence`.

## Railroads And Utilities

Look for:

- Hard-to-replicate infrastructure.
- Regulated or geographic scarcity.
- Predictable demand.
- Sensible allowed returns.
- Long asset lives.

Risks: regulation, labor, capex intensity, fuel/input costs, political pressure.

## Software And Digital Platforms

Look for:

- Mission-critical workflow.
- High switching costs.
- Net retention and pricing power.
- Low marginal cost.
- Strong ecosystem or data advantage.

Risks: disruption, customer concentration, SBC dilution, growth valued too aggressively, weak cash conversion.

## Semiconductors

Semiconductors require extra circle-of-competence discipline. Some companies have real moats; many are cyclical, capital-intensive, or exposed to fast technology transitions.

Quality signals:

- Structural role in an ecosystem: design software, equipment bottleneck, foundry scale, critical IP, packaging, or customer-qualified components.
- High switching cost or qualification burden for customers.
- Technology roadmap that compounds advantage rather than requiring value-destructive reinvestment.
- Gross margin and ROIC resilience across cycles.
- Customer diversification and long product cycles where possible.
- Balance sheet strong enough to invest through downturns.
- Evidence that demand is secular, not just inventory restocking.

Valuation cautions:

- Normalize earnings across semiconductor cycles.
- Separate peak-cycle margin from durable margin.
- Treat AI or hot-theme growth as uncertain until converted into cash flow and durable share.
- Do not call a company cheap on peak earnings.
- A great semiconductor business at a high multiple can be a watchlist candidate; define what price or normalized earnings would create a margin of safety.

Key red flags:

- Heavy capex with uncertain utilization.
- Customer concentration in a single device, cloud buyer, or geography.
- Export controls and geopolitical chokepoints.
- Rapid obsolescence or weak process roadmap.
- Commodity memory or foundry cycles mistaken for moats.
- Stock-based compensation or dilution hidden by adjusted metrics.

Cheapness traps to call out explicitly:

- Handset RF or component franchises with weak customer bargaining power can look cheap without having a durable moat.
- Turnaround fabs or lagging process stories can look statistically cheap while economics are deteriorating.
- Commodity memory names should not be called attractive on low multiples alone.
- If the thesis depends mainly on one customer, one node transition, or one hot cycle turning up, prefer `too hard`, `watchlist`, or `needs deeper underwriting`.

## Retail And Distribution

Look for:

- Cost advantage passed partly to customers.
- Inventory discipline.
- High turns and cash conversion.
- Scale purchasing.
- Culture of expense control.

Risks: low margins, e-commerce disruption, fashion/forecasting errors, leases, weak differentiation.

## Commodity And Cyclical Businesses

Be cautious. A low multiple near peak earnings is often a value trap.

Require:

- Low-cost position.
- Strong balance sheet.
- Conservative normalized earnings.
- Management that allocates countercyclically.
- Clear supply/demand discipline.

Often the right answer is "too hard" or "only at a deep cycle-adjusted discount."

Hard stop rules:

- If you cannot show a full-cycle view of earnings, cash flow, leverage, and survivability, do not call the stock attractive.
- If the thesis rests mainly on low PE, high dividend yield, or "it already fell a lot," treat that as a warning sign rather than evidence.

## Healthcare And Pharma

Look for:

- Durable patents or pipeline, but avoid overreliance on one product.
- Regulatory and reimbursement understanding.
- R&D productivity.
- Balance of innovation and capital returns.

Risks: patent cliffs, political pricing pressure, trial failure, acquisition-driven growth.
